SharpEye Internship

Summer 2026

Filming Stick Computer Vision Tracking Scoring Models & App

During the summer of 2026 I interned with SharpEye Performance, a startup building an AI tennis coaching app to bridge the gap between the coaching top players receive and what the average player working to get better has access to. Below are the three projects I worked on and my biggest takeaways from each.

Filming Stick

The filming stick started with brainstorming the problem and designing a product to solve it. Turning that idea into something real forced me to hone my CAD skills and learn to design around purchased parts, fitting my own components to hardware I didn't control.

From there I tested the prototype, made iterations based on what I found, and began learning about designing for manufacturability. My biggest takeaway was experiencing the full product design cycle, from defining a problem, to designing around real-world constraints, to iterating from a prototype toward something that can actually be manufactured.

Computer Vision Tracking

The computer vision project was an opportunity to learn as I went. I researched the landscape of object detection and tracking models and tested different options on our own footage to see what actually worked.

To train and evaluate the models, I built tools for manually labeling matches, creating our training and testing data. I then made adjustments to the models to maximize their performance. In the end we used different models for different jobs: one for court tracking, one for ball tracking, and one for player tracking. The most valuable lesson was learning to evaluate models against our own data rather than published benchmarks, and seeing how much the quality of labeled data drives performance. It also showed me that the best solution is often several specialized models working together rather than one model that does everything.

Scoring Models & App

This project built on the modeling I had done in class, but with a different goal. Instead of building a model to make predictions, I was building a metric that compares a player's performance to other players at a similar level.

I landed on using a linear regression to estimate expected performance, then used the distribution of residuals to turn how far a player landed above or below that expectation into a percentile rating. I also worked on the app where these ratings are shown to players.

This project pushed me to think about how much noise, luck, and randomness goes into a player's performance, an idea that runs against the common attitude in sports that every result is earned. More than anything, it taught me how to design a fair comparison metric and how to separate real skill from randomness in performance data.
