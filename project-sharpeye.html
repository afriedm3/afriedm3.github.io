SharpEye Internship

Summer 2026

Filming Stick Computer Vision Tracking Scoring Models & App

During the summer of 2026 I interned with SharpEye Performance, a startup building an AI tennis coaching app to bridge the gap between the coaching top players receive and what the average player working to get better has access to. I worked on three projects: the hardware that records matches, the computer vision that turns video into data, and the scoring models that turn that data into feedback.

1. Define the problem

SharpEye was recording on a basic fence pole. I started with a teardown of the leading competitor and similar mounts. Across reviews, the most common complaint was having to unclip the pole from the fence to change its height or angle. That set the target: adjustable angle without touching the mount, better wind stability, and charging through a two-to-three-hour match.

2. Concepts and requirements

We explored a motorized pole and a manual one with fence straps. I wrote the requirements for a third option: a hydraulic tilt, where a knob at the base of the pole drives a small piston at the head to angle the phone 0–30°. The final design combined it with a carbon fiber telescoping pole, a Qi charger behind the phone, a sun shield, and fold-out wings for stability.

3. Design around real parts

Turning the idea into something real forced me to hone my CAD skills and fit my own components to purchased hardware: the phone clamp, the charger board, and the threaded camera interface.

4. Check stability with numbers

We built a weight and moment budget for every component above, at, and below the fence hook. With the battery and grip at the base, the stabilizing moment came to 30,904 g·in against 7,395 g·in of tipping moment, about 4.2× more stable.

5. Cost and manufacturability

I priced the design at 100 and 1,000 units, with a target landed cost of $50–75 plus $4.5k–13k in one-time tooling. I also compared hydraulic and pneumatic tilt systems for the prototype (about $95–190 vs. $23–58) to see what the hydraulic version's reliability was costing us.

6. Prototype

The design was built into a working prototype and hung on a court fence to check fit, stability, and camera angle in real conditions.

Takeaway: I went through the full product design cycle, from defining a problem, to designing around real-world constraints, to iterating from a prototype toward something that can be manufactured.

1. Splitting broadcast video into points

The existing point splitter assumed a fixed camera. Broadcast footage cuts away to players, crowds, and replays between points, so I built a broadcast-aware segmenter that uses how much of the frame is court plus a ball detector to reject cutaways, and read the on-screen scoreboard with OCR to line points up with the hand-charted log.

2. Fixing the ball tracker

The ball tracker reported a detection on 99.6% of frames, and nearly all of them were wrong. I traced it to a conversion bug that silently reset the network's normalization layers on load. I replaced it with a natively trained PyTorch TrackNet model, which landed on the ball about 77–80% of the time during rallies.

3. Labeling and finding the data problem

I labeled every rally of a 98-rally match and fed the errors back into the splicer. That fixed seven clip windows and recovered two skipped points with zero regressions. It also showed that the public reference charts left out about 16% of points, mostly return errors.

4. Training the hit-timing model

The hit detector was trained only on broadcast labels and scored an F1 of 0.17 on held-out footage. I added frame-accurate ground truth from SwingVision matches, after correcting a timing offset, and F1 rose to 0.52 with recall going from 9% to 38%. Expanding to six SwingVision matches raised F1 from 0.42 to 0.55 on our test clip, and the team adopted it as the default model.

5. Making correction fast

A model doesn't need to be perfect if fixing its mistakes is fast. I built a browser tool that plays each rally next to a court map so a person can accept, nudge, add, or delete hits and bounces, and it logs the time spent per rally. With a good starting draft, 90% of shots were accepted unchanged at about 15 seconds per rally. With the old rule-based draft, the person had to rebuild the rally (43 additions, 42 deletions). I also automated court calibration with a RANSAC fit, to within 3–5 pixels.

6. Chasing false positives

The remaining errors were mostly false hits on the far side of the court. First I fixed how each hit is assigned to a player: measuring from the wrist instead of the feet raised near-side attribution accuracy from 47% to 69%. Then I tested five after-the-fact filters for removing false hits (timing, alternation, ball proximity, trajectory kinks, and bounce overlap). Each filter separated real hits from false ones no better than a coin flip (AUC ≈ 0.5), even though the ball was usually in view: most false hits happened with a player genuinely near the ball. That showed the fix had to come from better tracking and the detector itself, not cleanup rules, and the team took that direction.

7. Where the model ended up

Building on that groundwork, the team's hit detector reached about 89 F1 on SharpEye's standard camera setup and 80.5 F1 on camera setups it had never seen by mid-July, measured during live points against SwingVision's labels. SwingVision itself is reported at roughly 85–90% shot-detection accuracy, so this is close to parity.

Takeaway: Evaluate models against your own data, not published benchmarks. Label quality and quantity drove performance more than architecture, and the best system was several specialized models (court, ball, player, hits) working together.

1. Reframing the question

This built on modeling I'd done in class, but with a different goal. Instead of predicting outcomes, I was building a metric that compares a player's performance to what's expected at their level, so a player can hear "you served like a 16.2 UTR player today."

2. Building the dataset

I paired 10,339 player-match records from the Match Charting Project and SharpEye's own matches with each player's UTR as of the match date, scraped week by week. That meant handling problems like returning pros whose UTR had decayed during long layoffs.

3. Fitting the model

For each stat I fit stat% = c0 + c1·player UTR + c2·opponent UTR separately by gender. The data rejected a single "skill gap" term: serve and return effects are asymmetric because the server holds the bigger weapon. Men win 64.9% of serve points in an even match and women 57.6%, which is why gender stays in the model.

4. Separating skill from luck

A match's serve percentage comes from about 70 points, so I measured how much of the spread between matches is random chance: about 43% for serve and 52% for rallies. The residual is converted into UTR units and scaled down by that amount, which keeps the scale believable. For Clutch Factor, a single match turned out to be about 95% noise, so it's shown as a percentile instead of a precise rating.

5. Over time and in the app

I added ratings that track a player across matches using an exponentially weighted average, tuned on about 9,200 predictions. I then ported the model into SharpEye's production report pipeline: computing pressure points directly from the match log (including no-ad and tiebreak formats), adding a database migration to store residuals and player ratings, and updating each player's rating after every match. In the app, I built the Shot Map, which plots bounce locations on a to-scale court for web and iOS, and UTR auto-fill.

Takeaway: A lot of a single result is noise, which runs against the common attitude in sports that every result is earned. This project taught me how to design a fair comparison metric and how to separate real skill from randomness in performance data.
